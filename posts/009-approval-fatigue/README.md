# The human in the loop approves one in three attacks

*2026-08-07 · the RunAI Coder team · [run.ceo/coder](https://run.ceo/coder/?utm_source=github_Official_Page)*

Earlier this week a developer tools company called Scale X published data from a browser game with a grim premise: you are the human in the loop. An AI coding agent proposes shell commands, one after another, and you approve or deny each under time pressure. Some commands are routine (`git status`, `npm test`). About a third are attacks: credential reads, crontab persistence, exfiltration. Forty thousand sessions and [409,000 individual decisions](https://scalex.dev/blog/ai-agent-permissions-stats/) later, the average player had missed one in three threats.

Read that number charitably and it gets worse. These were players who knew they were being tested, engaged enough to play a security game, watching commands scroll past with nothing else competing for their attention. Production conditions subtract all three advantages: you don't know today is the day, you're mid-task in your own work, and the agent interrupts you mid-thought. The game is close to a best case, and the best case missed 33%.

## The misses aren't where you'd guess

The breakdown is the useful part. The obvious-destruction category, `rm -rf /` and its cousins, had an 11.7% miss rate. Scope violations like `cat ~/.aws/credentials` slipped past 35%. But the worst misses, by a wide margin, were malicious npm scripts: `npm run analyze` sailed through 64.7% of the time, and the pooled miss rate for disguised script invocations was 52.5%, against 28.4% for other exfiltration attempts.

The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because it indicts the whole interface. The approval prompt shows you a command string, and the command string is honest: you really are running the `analyze` script. The attack lives in `package.json`, one indirection away, where the prompt doesn't reach. The player answered exactly the question the UI asked, and the UI asked the wrong question.

Attention also bled in the other direction. Players blocked 45% of `rm -rf dist/` invocations, a perfectly normal build-cleanup command, and 59% of `npm config set registry` calls. All that vigilance spent policing routine commands came out of the same finite pool the npm attacks were quietly draining, in the same players, in the same session. And at the bottom of the distribution, 7% of players simply approved everything. That group matters most: they are the end state of the mechanism the rest of this piece is about.

## This doesn't train away

None of this is new to human-factors research; the new part is only that coding agents put an unpaid security-operations job inside every developer's terminal. [Parasuraman and Manzey's 2010 review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055) of automation complacency found the effect in novices and experts alike, showing up when the human is supervising automation while doing other work, and, critically, refusing to yield to simple practice. Complacency is not a skill gap. It's what attention allocation does under competing load.

The habituation numbers are more brutal. A BYU team spent years watching brains habituate to security warnings under fMRI: the visual system's response drops measurably by the second exposure and keeps declining from there. The one mitigation that held up in [their experiments](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2702123.2702322) was the polymorphic warning, a warning that keeps changing its appearance. Novelty kept the visual system's attention where identical warnings lost it.

Now count how many approval prompts your coding agent showed you yesterday. If it's a normal agentic session, the answer is dozens, near-identical, overwhelmingly benign. That is a habituation training program with excellent adherence. The 7% who approve everything are simply further along the same curve everyone is on. The community even named the destination: YOLO mode, running the agent with permission checks off, which as of August 2026 is common enough that the major harnesses ship a flag for it, next to a warning against using it.

## Attention is the budget; spend it where it verifies

The framing that made this actionable for us: an approval prompt is a purchase. It buys a slice of scarce human attention and spends it on one question. Most agent harnesses currently spend that budget on the cheapest possible question, "does this command string look scary," which the 409,000-decision dataset shows humans answer at close to chance for the attacks that matter.

Three spending rules follow, roughly in order of leverage.

**Stop buying what the sandbox gives you free.** A command that cannot reach your credentials, your network, or anything outside a scratch directory does not need a human decision at all. As of August 2026 the major harnesses ship sandbox runtimes and permission allowlists, but the defaults are permissive and the escape hatches are open; the community keeps publishing stricter wrappers around them, which tells you where the defaults should have been. Deny-listing sensitive paths, pinning network egress, and running the whole session in a container converts hundreds of approval prompts into zero, without a human blinking. One practitioner in the [HN thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49195468) described exactly this: the agent works in a throwaway scratch pad it can't leave, and moving results out is a deliberate human act. Irritating, yes, and vastly safer than spending attention.

**Move the decision to where evidence exists.** A commenter in that thread put the distinction better than any paper I've read: approval is a claim about the future, verification is a claim about a result. When you approve `npm run analyze`, you are predicting what code you haven't seen will do. When you review a finished change, a diff, the test run, the trace of what actually executed, you are checking something that already happened, with the receipts in front of you. Humans are measurably mediocre prophets; we think they make better auditors, though the game never tested that half. Our own shop runs on this bet: the agent works inside the sandbox without per-command interruptions, and human judgment is spent at the edges, a plan check before the consequential parts, then the delivery itself, over the diff and the test results. Dozens of per-command prophecies collapse into a handful of evidence-backed decisions, the kind a tired primate can actually answer.

**Make what remains rare and loud.** Some actions genuinely need a human before the fact: pushing to a shared branch, spending money, touching production. Those prompts should be rare enough to stay novel, because the fMRI work says novelty is most of the game, and they should carry the evidence a decision needs: the command plus where the instruction came from, what it reads, and where its output goes. A prompt that fires three times a day and looks different each time gets read. A prompt that fires forty times an hour trains you to stop seeing it, and then the one that mattered is number thirty-one.

## What this doesn't solve

The game overstates threat density: a third of its commands were hostile, while a real week might contain none, and the low-prevalence effect in vigilance research says [rarer targets get missed more often](https://www.nature.com/articles/435439a). So 66.3% accuracy is probably an optimistic ceiling, but the game's time pressure also cut the other way, and nobody has measured the production version. There is also a cynical reading of approval prompts, aired repeatedly in the thread: their real product is liability transfer, and "you approved it" is a sentence written for lawyers. We think that reading is partly right and changes nothing about the design conclusion; a control that exists for the lawyers still spends your attention. And the deeper fixes are open problems: capability-based security that would make "what can this code touch" a language-level property remains mostly unbuilt, and as long as an agent can make arbitrary web requests, exfiltration channels exist that no approval prompt will surface. Our delivery-gate practice is one shop's answer on one workload, and nobody has measured it beyond that.

The uncomfortable version: if your agent-safety story ends with "and then a human reviews it," the load-bearing component is a primate that stops seeing warnings on the second exposure. Budget its attention like you budget anything scarce. Let the sandbox eat the routine decisions, move the human to questions with evidence attached, and keep the true interrupts rare enough that they still look like news.
